# DELTA in The Last Judgment: How Sacred Art Can Illuminate the DELTA Framework

Delivered by **Elizabeth Lev**, Professor of Christian Art and Architecture at the Pontifical University of Saint Thomas Aquinas (Angelicum, Rome), on Tuesday, September 22, 2026, at the DELTA Summit on AI, Faith, and Human Flourishing.

---

## 1. Executive Summary & Core Theses

In this visual and theological tour-de-force, art historian Elizabeth Lev explores how sacred art rescues the DELTA framework from being treated as abstract intellectual theory. Focusing on Michelangelo Buonarroti's masterwork on the altar wall of the Sistine Chapel (*The Last Judgment*, unveiled 1541), Lev maps each letter of the **DELTA** acronym to Renaissance theological and artistic revolutions:

1. **D — Dignity (Judgment Presupposes Dignity)**:
   The very concept of a "Last Judgment" affirms human dignity: only creatures endowed with moral freedom and transcendent worth can have their choices evaluated as noble or wanting. Unlike medieval depictions that treated damnation as spectacle, Michelangelo emphasizes the desperate effort of grace to salvage every soul, referencing Pope Paul III’s 1537 encyclical *Sublimis Deus* prohibiting the enslavement of indigenous peoples.
2. **E — Embodiment (The Human Form Sublime)**:
   Michelangelo’s poetry reveals his artistic premise: *"Nowhere else has God deigned to show himself more / than in human form sublime, which since they image him, they alone I adore."* In an age of disembodied algorithms and silicon chips, Christian art insists that our physical, vulnerable bodies are heaven’s "trophy" athletes—the sacred locus of *Imago Dei*.
3. **L — Love (The Generating Center)**:
   Love occupies the central position of the DELTA acronym, echoing Dante’s closing line of the *Divine Comedy*: *"L'amor che move il sole e l'altre stelle"* (the love that moves the sun and the other stars). Michelangelo’s Christ is not a petty punisher, but an overwhelming, muscular force of cosmic, restorative love, balanced by the Virgin Mary as the intercessor.
4. **T — Transcendence (Art as a Horizon Beyond Itself)**:
   Western sacred art never functioned as an end in itself; its purpose is to evoke awe, prayer, and humility before a mystery that exceeds human intellect. St. John Paul II declared the Sistine Chapel *"the sanctuary of the theology of the human body."*
5. **A — Agency (The Freedom to Choose Good)**:
   Drawing upon Matthew 25 (*"For I was hungry and you gave me food..."*), Michelangelo portrays human bodies actively assisting one another, lifting souls by the rosary, and celebrating female martyrs whose moral agency defied imperial coercion.

## 4. The 5 DELTA Pillars in Sacred Art

### 4.1. Dignity: The Premise of Moral Accountability
Lev argues that modern technocratic discourse frequently misunderstands the nature of judgment:
> *"The mere fact that there is a last judgment implies that the human person is invested with dignity and freedom—that their choices and their actions can be judged as excellent or wanting, unlike the actions of squirrels and palm trees."* `[16:56]`

Michelangelo explicitly broke from the medieval Tuscan tradition (such as Giotto at the Arena Chapel) that lingered sadistically over tormenting demons. Instead, Michelangelo’s composition focuses on the drama of redemption: angels battling demons to rescue wavering souls, and the depiction of newly encountered indigenous souls being drawn upward with a rosary, reflecting Pope Paul III’s 1537 bull *Sublimis Deus* asserting the dignity and inviolable freedom of indigenous peoples.

### 4.2. Embodiment: Heaven’s Athletes
In an era where tech evangelists champion "transhumanism"—a term originally coined by Dante (*trasumanar* in *Paradiso* I)—to describe shedding the flesh, Christian art asserts the resurrection of the physical body:
* **Heaven’s Athletes**: Drawing upon Greco-Roman athletic statues, martyrs are shown holding palm fronds not as pathetic victims, but as victorious champions who crossed heaven's finish line through bodily endurance.
* **The Flayed Skin of St. Bartholomew**: The saint holds his flayed skin, bearing the distorted face of Michelangelo himself—a visual admission that the artist's fragile, failing flesh cannot save itself, but relies entirely on Christ’s transfiguring mercy.

### 4.3. Love: The Cosmic Engine
Positioned at the literal center of both the DELTA acronym and Dante's *Commedia*, love is the generative power that fuels creation:
* Michelangelo lived an ascetic, solitary life, famously declaring that his art was his wife and his works were his children. Under his irritable exterior lay a passionate, vibrating devotion.
* **"Terminator Jesus"**: Christ is portrayed without a beard, powerfully athletic, rising in judgment. Lev notes that this terrifying physicality represents the irresistible, disruptive reality of divine love—a love that refuses to leave creation corrupted by injustice.

### 4.4. Transcendence: The Horizon Beyond the Screen
Transcendence distinguishes human contemplation from computational processing:
* Ancient Egyptian, Greek, Roman, and Christian art all agreed that technique is not an end in itself; breathtaking beauty exists to draw the viewer outward into prayer and reverence.
* Quoting St. John Paul II, the Sistine Chapel is *"the sanctuary of the theology of the human body,"* visually demonstrating that the visible material world points toward invisible divine glory.

### 4.5. Agency: The Moral Heroism of Matthew 25
Michelangelo’s figures are not passive chess pieces; they exercise active agency:
* **The Corporal Works of Mercy**: Souls actively haul one another upward, mirroring Matthew 25: *"I was hungry and you gave me food."*
* **Female Moral Agency**: Michelangelo’s female figures (sibyls and martyrs) are depicted with muscular, heroic physiques. Rejecting decorative passivity, Michelangelo presents women as spiritual titans who actively chose martyrdom over submission to imperial Roman decree.
