# DELTA Summit 2026: Opening Dinner & Presidential Address

Delivered on Monday, September 21, 2026, at the University of Notre Dame, following the Opening Mass at the Basilica of the Sacred Heart.

---

## 1. Executive Summary & Core Theses

At the Opening Dinner of the inaugural **DELTA Summit on AI, Faith, and Human Flourishing**, **Rev. Robert A. Dowd, C.S.C.**, 18th President of the University of Notre Dame, formally welcomed global delegates and set the strategic charter for the multi-day gathering.

Fr. Dowd framed the Summit around the **Notre Dame Forum** university-wide theme—*"What does it mean to live a good life?"*—and articulated three foundational leadership imperatives for technological governance:

1. **Urgency of Moral Action**: While contemplation and academic rigor are indispensable, the sheer speed of AI deployment requires decisive moral action. Catholic research universities and faith communities cannot remain passive observers; they must actively shape technological trajectories.
2. **The DELTA Pillars & *Magnifica Humanitas***: Echoing Cardinal Cupich's homily and the papal doctrine articulated in *Magnifica Humanitas*, Fr. Dowd highlighted the core DELTA pillars:
   * **Dignity** (*Imago Dei*): Inviolable worth that cannot be reduced to data doubles or productivity scores.
   * **Embodiment**: Grounding human experience in physical presence, vulnerability, and face-to-face communion.
   * **Love**: Prioritizing relational care and charity over transactional efficiency.
   * **Transcendence**: Protecting openness to the sacred and the divine initiative.
   * **Agency**: Ensuring human beings remain the conscious moral authors of their choices and institutions.
3. **The Non-Offloading Imperative**: Society faces a grave moral hazard: the temptation to "offload" accountability, judgment, and ethical limits to automated algorithmic systems. Fr. Dowd called for human ownership over the boundaries of AI, specifically in **surveillance, biological research, warfare, and the dignity of human work**.

## 4. Key Takeaways & Philosophical Insights

### 4.1. The Good Life in an Algorithmic Age
Fr. Dowd ties the Summit directly to the intellectual tradition of virtue ethics and Aristotle's *eudaimonia*:
* The classical question of the good life is made urgent by generative technologies that attempt to automate cognitive, creative, and moral discernment.
* A "good life" cannot be measured by algorithmic efficiency; it requires cultivating **uniquely human qualities**—moral imagination, contemplation, empathy, and sacrifice.

### 4.2. Refusing to "Offload" Moral Accountability
A central warning of Fr. Dowd's address is the psychological and institutional temptation of moral abdication:
> *"It would be very easy for us to offload our responsibilities to AI or to others. But we have a very special responsibility. We must take ownership of the limits that should govern AI in surveillance, biological research, warfare, and other areas. We must remain steadfast in our commitment to creating systems and structures that protect and honor the dignity of the human person, the dignity of work."* `[24:47]`

Delegating life-and-death decisions, surveillance criteria, or the dignity of human labor to automated optimization algorithms constitutes an abandonment of moral agency.

### 4.3. The Broad Ecumenical and Multidisciplinary Tent
Fr. Dowd highlights that answering the challenge of AI cannot be done within disciplinary silos:
* **Technologists & Frontier Labs**: Must sit with theologians and moral philosophers.
* **Pastoral Ministers & Educators**: Must translate ethical frameworks into real-world school settings and parishes.
* **Youth & Emerging Leaders**: Must not merely be subjects of adult policymaking, but active co-designers of the technological environment.
